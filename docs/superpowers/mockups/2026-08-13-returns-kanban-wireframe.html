Returns, as a desk and a board

A wireframe for the returns queue: what needs a person, and where everything is. Static — nothing here clicks. Drawn in the application's own tokens, with the board borrowing Trello's shape because Trello solved this arrangement and an operator already knows how to read it.

The structural decision everything follows from. “Needs action” is not a stage — it is a judgement about a card. A column is a place a card is, and a card can only be in one place, so needs-action cannot be a column without lying about where the work sits. It becomes a table above the board. The desk answers “what do I do next?”; the board answers “where is everything?” A card legitimately appears in both.
A district is a board, not a lane. The expensive decision here is which van goes where today, and that decision is made one district at a time — so Maitama is a board, Wuse II is a board, and the Requested list on a board is the dispatch list for that district. Its header carries the total spools, which is what decides whether one van is enough. The picker in the top right is how you move between them, and it doubles as the answer to “where is there work at all”.
Abuja only, and the boards are the reason it stays honest. A spool comes back because a driver fetches it, so the programme is served where there are drivers — Abuja, today. Every board is a served district; an address that resolves to none of them cannot be given a board, and a return that has no board can never reach Received, which is the only state that awards. The geography is not a rule written down somewhere and checked later: it is the shape of the screen.

One district, one board. The board shows Maitama and only Maitama; the other districts are boards of their own, reached from the picker in the header. The desk above it is the exception and stays whole-of-Abuja on purpose — “what needs me” is a question about the day, not about a district, and an operator should not have to visit six boards to discover that two of them are on fire. Its Area column is how a row on the desk tells you which board it lives on. Every list is a fixed width, so nothing reflows when a card moves — the whole point of a board is that things stay where you put them. “＋ Log a return” appears under Requested only, because that is the one list a return can be born into; the others are reached by moving. Returns whose address resolves to no served district get no board at all — they sit in the picker's red footer, where they can be cancelled or rejected but never awarded.

The badge inverts by construction. It is --ink on --paper: a near-black disc with light type in the light theme, and — because those two tokens swap — the white disc with black type in the dark one. One rule, both themes, no second colour to maintain.

Idle districts stay listed, greyed. Removing them would read as “we do not serve Jabi”, which is a different and much worse claim than “nothing is waiting in Jabi today”. They are still reachable from search and from a desk row; they are just not a place the picker will send you for no reason.

A3 · Abuja only, and what happens outside it

Spool points are earned by returning spools, and a spool is returned by a driver who has to physically get to it. So the programme is served in Abuja and nowhere else — which has to be said before somebody packs a box, not after.

⚑
We only collect in Abuja right now

Your address is in Port Harcourt, and we have no driver there yet. You can still buy filament — there is just nothing to earn for sending the spools back until we reach you.

Use a different address Tell me when you reach me

Served today: Maitama, Wuse, Wuse II, Garki, Garki II, Asokoro, Jabi, Utako, Gwarinpa and 12 more.

The gate is at the door, not at the till. The check runs when the address is entered — on the storefront's request form and on the admin's “Log a return…” — so nobody schedules a van they cannot send, and no customer is told “no” after they have already boxed twelve spools.

Out-of-area returns still exist, they just cannot pay. If one is logged anyway — a phone call, a walk-in from out of town — it lands in the picker's red footer rather than on a district board. It can be cancelled or rejected with a reason; what it cannot do is award, because no board can move it to Received.

The drop is the intent; the form is the act. Releasing here does not silently move the card — a pickup needs a date and a driver, so the schedule form opens with the card held in place, and the move commits when the form does. The same is true of Received, which needs counted quantities and cannot be completed by a gesture. Lists that would refuse the card are dimmed while it is in hand, and a card dropped on one springs back with a line saying which rule stopped it.

F · The card, annotated

Dara Adeyemi
6 spools
4d 6h→ 60 ptsDA

Four lines, none needing a second glance to decode.

1
The label bar. Trello's coloured strip, doing real work: it is the waiting band — red past 96h, amber past 48h, nothing when nothing is wrong. It is what makes a late card findable from the far side of the board. This replaces the dots that used to sit beside the column names: the state belongs to the card, not to the column, because every card in a column has a different age.
2
Who. Their name when there is one, their checkout email when there is not. Returns are keyed on email, so an address is never wrong — only less friendly.
3
The load, in the programme's own words. “6 spools” is rendered from the programme's unit labels — rename it to canisters and every card says canisters. The number decides van capacity, so it is the boldest thing after the name.
4
The wait, again, in words. Colour is never the only carrier: “4d 6h” says it for anyone who cannot separate the red bar from the amber one.
5
What it is worth. Quantity × the rate promised when it was requested, so a 12-spool return is visibly worth ten times a small one before you decide whose van to fill.
6
The monogram. Where Trello puts a member's avatar we put the customer's initials — this app has no member photos, and two letters are enough to tell two rows apart while scanning.

G · Where the districts come from

Checked against the real service before writing it down: Overpass, OpenStreetMap's query API. Free, no key, no account. This query returns Abuja's districts by name.

POST https://overpass-api.de/api/interpreter
[out:json][timeout:80]; node["place"~"^(suburb|quarter|neighbourhood|district)$"](8.90,7.30,9.15,7.60); out tags;

What it actually returned, run on 13 August: Maitama District · Wuse District · Wuse II District · Garki · Garki II District · Asokoro District · Central Business District · Guzape District · Gudu District · Durumi District · Jabi District · Jahi District · Kado District · Katampe District · Kaura District · Kukwaba District · Mabushi District · Utako District · Wuye District · Dakibiyu District · Gwarinpa — plus a little noise (“CBN Quaters Lugbe Phase 2”, “NCCE Quarters Karmo”) that a human should strike out once.

Fetch it once, not on every request. Overpass is a shared public service with no uptime promise — one of the two calls made while writing this came back “the server is probably too busy”, which is a fine thing to meet in a terminal and a terrible thing to meet in a customer's address form. So the list is pulled once, curated by hand, and checked in as the served-districts config the admin can edit. It is business data anyway: which districts you serve is a decision about drivers, not a fact about geography, and the day you stop covering Kubwa you edit a list rather than wait for OpenStreetMap to agree with you.

Two things the query does not give you, so add them by hand: Wuse Zone 1–7, which locals use constantly and OSM files under Wuse; and the aliases people actually type — “Wuse 2”, “Wuse II”, “VI” — which the address matcher needs so a customer is not told they are outside Abuja for spelling it their own way.

What the board must not become. Dragging is a state transition, and two of these cannot be completed by a gesture: inspecting needs counted quantities and rejecting needs a reason. So a drop opens the form that finishes the move rather than moving the card silently — and if the form is abandoned, the card goes back where it was. A board that lets a gesture award points is a board that will award points by accident.

Not drawn, deliberately: the filter menus' contents, the settings behind a programme, and the inspection form itself — that one exists already and is not changing. This wireframe is about where a return lives, how it moves, and how many can move at once.
